# The Science Behind Studiolo

Every rule in this system is based on a finding that researchers have replicated many times.
This page covers each rule, why it works, and where it comes from. There's nothing here about
learning styles or brain myths, and no made-up numbers. It's all standard textbook learning
science.

## Produce, don't just read

You learn by pulling things out of your memory, not by putting more in. Rereading and
highlighting put more in. They feel good and make the material familiar, but they don't build
much memory. Testing yourself pulls it out, and pulling it out is what makes it stick.

- Roediger & Karpicke (2006), *Psychological Science*: students who took recall tests remembered
  much more a week later than students who reread the same text.
- Karpicke & Blunt (2011), *Science*: plain free recall beat concept mapping. Closing the book
  and writing down what you remember worked better than the fancier method.
- Dunlosky et al. (2013), *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*: out of ten common study
  techniques, only practice testing and spreading practice out over time got the top rating.
  Rereading and highlighting got the lowest.

That's why Studiolo's daily session (recall from memory, try the task, explain it) uses testing
as the lesson itself, not as a check at the end.

## Spread it out

The same number of hours spread over several days works better than the same hours all at once.
The review schedule (1, 3, 7, and 14 days, going up to 30 and 60 days for topics at level 4 and
5) brings each topic back around the time you'd start to forget it. Remembering something after
a longer gap makes the memory stronger than an easy review the next morning. That's also why
only high levels get long gaps. If you stretch the gap on something you barely know, you'll come
back to it and remember nothing.

- Cepeda et al. (2006), *Psychological Bulletin*: a review of hundreds of spacing experiments.
  Spacing beat cramming in nearly all of them.

## Struggling helps

Researchers call difficulty you can get through *desirable*. The harder it is to remember
something, the stronger the memory gets, as long as you get there in the end. That's why the
system has you try things before any explanation, gives you time to struggle before you can ask
for help, and has you break things on purpose. Even a failed attempt before the lesson helps
you learn more from it.

- Bjork (1994), in *Metacognition: Knowing about knowing*: desirable difficulties, and the false
  sense of knowing that easy studying creates.
- Richland, Kornell & Kao (2009), *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied*: trying and
  failing before being taught (pretesting) improved later learning.
- Kornell, Hays & Bjork (2009), *Journal of Experimental Psychology: LMC*: failed attempts to
  remember improved what people learned afterwards.

The wrong-twice rule for flashcards, the 10-minute struggle time, and showing only 3 overdue
reviews on a backlog day are choices this system makes based on these findings. The research
says struggle helps. Studiolo picks amounts that are hard enough to help but not so hard that
you give up and don't come back tomorrow.

## Explain it to learn it

Following an explanation isn't the same as being able to give one. The Listener role (a voice
note, a sketch, and five written lines) is the most active kind of learning in the ICAP
framework. Working through it with someone beats producing it yourself, producing it yourself
beats doing something with the material, and doing something beats just reading or listening.

- Chi & Wylie (2014), *Educational Psychologist*: the ICAP framework. Explaining and working it
  out together lead to deeper learning than receiving information or handling it.

## New procedures: examples first, then less help

For skills with steps (an integral, a git rebase, a verb form), beginners learn best by studying
a fully worked example, then finishing a half-done one, then solving one with some steps
missing, and only then solving one alone. As you get better, the examples stop helping and plain
problems work better. That's the **Example ladder** in `labs/practice-library.md`. You climb it
the first time you meet a procedure. After that you solve a new version from scratch.

- Sweller & Cooper (1985), *Cognition and Instruction*: beginners learning a procedure did better
  studying worked examples than solving problems.
- Kalyuga et al. (2003), *Educational Psychologist*: the expertise reversal effect. The same help
  that lifts beginners up holds more experienced learners back.

## What Studiolo doesn't use

- **Learning styles** ("I'm a visual learner"). Matching teaching to someone's supposed style has
  failed every controlled test. Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork (2008), *Psychological Science
  in the Public Interest*. The practice app's visual drills test whether you can rebuild a
  structure from memory. They aren't there to suit anyone's style.
- **Made-up numbers.** This page doesn't quote any effect sizes, on purpose. The system's rule
  about never stating a number you're unsure of applies to the system too. The starter practice
  questions link straight to the papers they rely on.
