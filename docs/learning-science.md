# The Science Behind Studiolo

Every rule in this system comes from a finding that has been repeated many times. This page
is the short version: the rule, why it works, and where it comes from. No learning styles,
no brain myths, and no made-up numbers — every claim here is textbook-level learning science.

## Produce, don't consume

The core claim: you learn by pulling knowledge out of your head, not by pushing it in.
Rereading and highlighting push in. They feel good and build familiarity, not memory. Testing
yourself pulls out — and the pulling is what makes it stay.

- Roediger & Karpicke (2006), *Psychological Science*: students who took recall tests
  remembered far more a week later than students who reread the same text.
- Karpicke & Blunt (2011), *Science*: plain free recall beat concept mapping. "Close the
  book and write what you remember" beat the fancier study method.
- Dunlosky et al. (2013), *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*: of ten common
  study techniques, only practice testing and distributed practice got the top rating.
  Rereading and highlighting got the bottom one.

Studiolo's daily loop (recall cold → attempt → produce) uses testing as the lesson itself,
not as a check afterwards.

## Space it out

The same total hours, spread over days, beat the same hours crammed together. The review
ladder (1, 3, 7, 14 — stretching to 30 and 60 days for topics proven at level 4 and 5)
schedules each return just as forgetting starts. A longer gap that you survive makes a
stronger memory than an easy win the next morning. That is why only high levels earn long
gaps: stretch the gap on weak knowledge and you come back to a blank page.

- Cepeda et al. (2006), *Psychological Bulletin*: a review of hundreds of spacing
  experiments — spacing beat cramming in nearly all of them.

## Struggle is the point

Difficulty that you overcome is *desirable*: the harder the retrieval feels, the stronger
the trace, as long as you get there in the end. That is why the system makes you attempt
cold, holds the struggle window before any explanation, and makes breaking things part of
practice. Even a failed attempt before the lesson improves what you learn from it.

- Bjork (1994), in *Metacognition: Knowing about knowing*: desirable difficulties, and the
  illusions of knowing that easy study creates.
- Richland, Kornell & Kao (2009), *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied*: trying and
  failing before instruction (pretesting) improved later learning.
- Kornell, Hays & Bjork (2009), *Journal of Experimental Psychology: LMC*: unsuccessful
  retrieval attempts enhanced what was learned afterwards.

The wrong-twice flashcard threshold, the 10-minute struggle window, and backlog amnesty are
design choices that follow from these findings. The studies say "struggle helps"; Studiolo
picks the doses that keep a learner coming back tomorrow.

## Explain it to learn it

Being able to follow an explanation is not the same as giving one. The Listener role
(voice note + sketch + five written lines) sits at the top of the ICAP ladder: interactive
work beats constructive beats active beats passive.

- Chi & Wylie (2014), *Educational Psychologist*: the ICAP framework — explaining and
  co-constructing produce deeper learning than receiving or manipulating.

## New procedures: examples first, then fade

For skills with steps (an integral, a git rebase, a verb form), novices learn best by
studying a fully worked example, then completing a partial one, then solving with faded
hints — before solving cold. As skill grows, the same examples stop helping and problems
take over. That is the **Example ladder** in `labs/practice-library.md`: the first meeting
climbs it, later passes solve a fresh variant cold.

- Sweller & Cooper (1985), *Cognition and Instruction*: worked examples beat solving for
  novices learning a procedure.
- Kalyuga et al. (2003), *Educational Psychologist*: the expertise reversal effect — the
  same help that lifts beginners holds stronger learners back.

## What Studiolo refuses to build on

- **Learning styles** ("I'm a visual learner"): matching teaching to a diagnosed style has
  failed every controlled test. Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork (2008), *Psychological
  Science in the Public Interest*. The practice app's visual drills test whether you can
  rebuild a structure from memory. They are not there to match anyone's style.
- **Made-up numbers.** No effect sizes are quoted on this page on purpose. The system's own
  rule — never state a number you're unsure of — applies to the system itself. Practice
  items that teach this material carry `provenance` pointing back to this page.
